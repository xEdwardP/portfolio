## The problem

Charging for time looks like simple arithmetic until you hit the real edges of a parking lot.
Do you bill a full hour to someone leaving at 62 minutes? What about someone who leaves a car
for four days — four days of hourly rate?

A system that answers those two questions badly generates disputes at the counter every day.
And the rules cannot live in the cashier's head: they have to live in the system, or every
shift charges differently.

## Architecture decisions

### The grace period scales with the stay

Hourly billing applies a grace period that **grows with elapsed time**:

| Elapsed hours | Grace      |
| ------------- | ---------- |
| 1–8           | 10 minutes |
| 9–18          | 15 minutes |
| 19–23         | 20 minutes |

The reasoning is proportional: a few extra minutes weigh heavily on a short stay and barely
at all on a long one. A fixed 10-minute grace would be unfair at the top; a fixed 20 would be
giving money away at the bottom.

### The rate corrects itself

If a stay spans one or more full days, the ticket **switches automatically to the daily
rate** on close. The customer who leaves a car over the weekend does not pay 72 loose hours.

That is a business rule embedded in ticket closing, not a decision left to the operator.

### State lives on the ticket, not on the screen

Opening a ticket requires an existing space, vehicle and rate, and is rejected if the vehicle
already has an `activo` one — the validation that prevents billing the same car twice. The
customer is **derived from the vehicle**, never typed in, so there is no way to attach a stay
to the wrong customer.

The space flips to `ocupado` in the same operation, and the ticket number is generated
sequentially. While the vehicle is inside, an endpoint returns a live estimate of the amount
due so the counter can check it without closing anything.

## Outcome

End-to-end parking operation: customer and vehicle registration, space assignment, ticket
opening and closing, time-based rates, QR-coded invoices, and daily, weekly and monthly PDF
reports.

All behind authentication, with roles and permissions separating who operates the counter
from who reads the reports.
