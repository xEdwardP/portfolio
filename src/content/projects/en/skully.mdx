## The problem

An organisation handing out laptops, monitors and licences always ends up in the same place:
three spreadsheets that disagree, nobody sure who has the machine bought two years ago, and
a licence that expired last month without anyone noticing.

The inventory is the easy part. The hard part is everything around it: who handed it over,
in what condition, why it went into maintenance, how many seats of a licence are still free
and, above all, who is allowed to touch what.

## Architecture decisions

### Four layers and one rule that never bends

Dependencies always point inward: **Presentation → Application → Domain**, with
Infrastructure implementing the interfaces the inner layers define.

The proof that the rule holds is measurable: **`Skully.Domain` has not a single NuGet
dependency**. Its 19 entities, enums and repository contracts have no idea that Entity
Framework, Windows Forms or QuestPDF exist. Swapping the ORM or the UI would not force a
change in the domain.

### One use case per operation

Instead of services that grow until they hold twenty methods, there are **82 classes with a
single `ExecuteAsync`**. `CreateAssetUseCase` creates an asset and does nothing else.

It sounds excessive right up until something has to change: the class you edit is exactly
the one that matters, and so is its unit test.

### Expected failures are not exceptions

Every use case returns `Result<T>`. An asset already being assigned is not a program fault.
It is a perfectly foreseen business answer, and it travels as data all the way to the UI,
which displays it.

Exceptions stay reserved for what genuinely was not expected. It is the same judgement I
applied in Orders with `ActionResponse<T>`, and the reason neither application has defensive
`try/catch` blocks scattered through the presentation layer.

### Nothing is ever deleted

`BaseEntity` carries `DeletedAt`, and the generic repository marks records instead of
removing them. Every query filters on `DeletedAt == null`.

The detail that usually breaks this pattern is unique indexes: a deleted asset would hold on
to its serial number forever. The fix is **filtered** indexes, with
`HasFilter("[DeletedAt] IS NULL")`, so a deleted record frees the slot without vanishing
from the database. And no foreign key cascades: every relationship is `Restrict`, so
referential integrity is a decision the use case makes, not the engine.

### Permissions down to the operation, not the screen

The matrix is **12 modules by 6 permissions** (show, save, update, delete, print and
reprint) for each role, edited from inside the application without recompiling.

The UI honours it at two levels: sidebar buttons disappear when the show permission is
missing, and action buttons are disabled one by one. A consultant never sees a form they
cannot use.

### An audit trail that does not rely on remembering

`SaveChangesAsync` stamps `CreatedAt` and `UpdatedAt` on every save, and each asset status
change writes a row into `AssetStatusHistory` with the previous state, the new one, the
reason, who did it and when. There is no path that changes a status and skips the log.

## Outcome

Assets with six lifecycle states, internal components and peripherals, assignments with
returns, licences with seat tracking, a maintenance log, and four PDF reports with filters
and the company header.

The dashboard answers the question that actually matters on a Monday morning: what is in
maintenance, how many assignments are open, and which licences expire in the next thirty
days.

**82 unit tests**, concentrated where the business risk is highest: creating assets and
assignments, returns, authentication, licence assignment and revocation, and every deletion
path with its referential-integrity check. The tests stand in for `IUnitOfWork`, `IMapper`
and `IPasswordHasher`, so they touch neither the database nor the UI.

> This is the project where Repository and Unit of Work show up in their fullest form: a
> single `DbContext`, 19 repositories and one `SaveChangesAsync` per operation.
